Network · Follow up

After a Recruiting Event

Turn a short conversation into a professional relationship that lasts.

6 min read · Applies BUAD 302 skills

When to use this

You just met someone at a career fair, info session, panel, club event or coffee chat, and you want them to remember you for the right reasons.

Goal

Send one short, specific message that shows you listened, and keep a simple record so the contact doesn’t go cold.

Where BUAD 302 comes in

  • Audience analysis. A recruiter who met 200 students today and an alum who talked with you for 20 minutes need different messages. Decide who you’re writing to before you write.
  • Persuasion. A follow-up works when it’s easy to say yes to. A small, specific, optional ask beats a big, vague one.
  • Professional writing. Clear subject line, one idea per paragraph, short enough to read on a phone.

Steps

1. Same day: write down what you’ll forget

On the walk home, put these in your phone’s notes:

  • Name, role and organization
  • What you talked about, in a phrase
  • One specific thing they said that stuck with you
  • Anything you said you would do (“I’ll look into that program”)

Keep these notes on your own device. This site doesn’t collect or store them.

2. Within a day or two: send a short thank-you

Use the channel you exchanged: email if you got a card or address, LinkedIn if that’s how you connected. Mention one real detail. One day is a good target, not a hard rule. A thoughtful note on day four beats a generic one sent within the hour.

3. Do what you said you’d do

If you promised to read something, apply or attend another event, do it. Following through gives you a real reason for your next message.

4. Make one small ask, only if it fits

Good asks are optional and specific: one question, or whether they’d recommend a person or resource. Never treat an introduction or referral as owed.

5. Reconnect when you have a real reason

You applied their advice, got an interview at their organization, or found something relevant to what you discussed. Those are reasons. A monthly check-in with nothing to say is not.

6. Keep a simple record

A note or spreadsheet with four columns is enough: person, where you met, last contact, next reason to reach out.

Build your message

Fill in the fields and the preview updates as you type. Then copy the text into your email or LinkedIn.

Sending by

Write it so it finishes the sentence "I especially appreciated…"

Nothing you type is sent or stored. It disappears when you leave the page.

Weak vs. strong

Illustrative example, not a real exchange.

Weak: “Thanks for chatting at the career fair! It was great meeting you and learning about your company. Hope to stay in touch!”

Stronger: “Thank you for taking the time to talk with me at the fall career fair. I especially appreciated your advice to talk to people on more than one team before applying. I had only been looking at one, so I’m widening my search. If there is anyone you would recommend I speak with, I would be grateful for the suggestion.”

The weak version could go to anyone. The stronger one proves you listened and offers one easy, optional next step.

How to adapt this to your situation

Who you’re writing to

  • Recruiter: They meet many students. Be brief, name the role or program you discussed, and don’t ask for things they can’t give.
  • Alum or working professional: More room for curiosity. A question about their path fits here.
  • Senior leader: Shortest version. Thank them and mention one detail. No ask.
  • Fellow student: Keep it casual and offer something back, like a resource or an upcoming event.

How long you talked

  • Two minutes in a line: thank them and mention one detail. Skip the ask.
  • Twenty minutes one-on-one: a specific question or a request for a recommendation is reasonable.

If something didn’t go to plan

  • You forgot the detail. Don’t invent one. Refer to the topic in general terms instead.
  • More than a week has passed. Send it anyway. Open with “I wanted to follow up on our conversation at…” and skip the long apology.
  • You have no contact information. A LinkedIn connection request with a short note works. If you can’t find them, let it go.
  • They don’t reply. That’s normal and isn’t a rejection. Don’t nudge them about the same message. Reach out again only when you have a new, real reason.

Common mistakes

  • Sending the same message to everyone you met. Colleagues may compare notes.
  • Asking for a job, referral or interview in the first message.
  • Attaching your resume when nobody asked for it.
  • Writing too much. If it needs scrolling on a phone, cut it.
  • Flattering (“Your talk was amazing!”) instead of being specific.
  • Following up again and again when they haven’t replied.
  • Keeping no notes, then forgetting who said what.
