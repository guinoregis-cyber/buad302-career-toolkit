How this connects to BUAD 302
  • Audience analysis. When you reach out to someone, they will probably look at your profile. Write it for that reader: a recruiter or alum skimming to see who you are and what you’re pursuing.
  • Credibility. Persuasion depends on credibility. An accurate, consistent profile supports every message you send.
  • Professional writing. A headline and a short “About” section are professional writing: clear, specific, no filler.

LinkedIn is one source, not the only one. Profiles are written by their owners and leave things out. Check what matters against other sources: your university’s alumni directory, employers’ own websites, professional associations in the field, and people you meet at events.

The four steps

1. Make your profile credible

This isn’t a branding exercise. Your resume work from class does most of it. Check what someone sees in the first few seconds:

  • A clear photo of you.
  • A headline that says who you are and what you’re exploring. For example: “Business administration student at USC, interested in corporate finance and consulting.”
  • Experience that matches your resume: same titles, dates and facts. Inconsistencies cost trust.
  • Nothing exaggerated. Anyone you worked with could read it.
  • A short “About” section in your own words: what you study, what you’re exploring and why.

Accurate and clear beats polished. Don’t spend hours on it.

2. Explore career paths

Pick a role you’re curious about and look at five to ten people who have it. Look for patterns:

  • What did they do in their first two or three years?
  • Which degrees, earlier roles and skills keep showing up?
  • How long did they stay, and where did they go next?

Each profile is one example, not a requirement. Do the same for organizations: what roles their people hold, where those people came from, and what the organization says about its own work.

3. Identify the right people

The most useful person is often not the most senior. Someone a few years ahead of you remembers recruiting and is more likely to have time. Look for a path similar to the one you’re considering, a specific career switch you want to understand, or a shared school or group.

For each person, write down one specific question their profile doesn’t answer.

4. Connect with a reason

Use what you learned as your reason for reaching out, then follow Reach Out to Someone New. If you’ve already met them, use After a Recruiting Event. A connection request without a note is easy to ignore. If you add one, lead with your reason.

Seen an opening? Treat any posting as a lead, then confirm it on the employer’s own careers page before applying. Be wary of any “opportunity” that asks for payment or financial information.

Weak vs. strong

Illustrative example.

Weak reason: “I saw you work at a tech company and I’m interested in tech.”

Stronger reason: “I noticed that you moved from audit into corporate development after a few years, and as someone exploring corporate finance, I am trying to understand what skills made that move possible.”

The weak one could apply to thousands of people. The stronger one shows you looked at their path and have a question only someone like them can answer.

How to adapt this to your situation

The four steps don’t change. How you use them does. Open the sections that match your situation.

A. What is your goal right now?
  • Exploring a career. Look at many profiles, note patterns, and collect questions. You don’t need to contact anyone yet.
  • Researching a specific role. Compare people in the same role at different organizations. What’s common, and what’s specific to one place?
  • Researching an organization. Look at the backgrounds of people on the teams that interest you, then check what you learned against the organization’s own website.
  • Finding people to contact. Shortlist three to five people, each with a specific question.
  • Looking for openings. Treat postings as leads and verify each one on the employer’s site.
B. Who are you looking at?
  • Recent graduate (a few years out). Best for questions about recruiting and what the first year is really like.
  • Mid-career professional. Best for how the field works and how paths split over time.
  • Recruiter. Best for process questions. Their profile often shows which roles they hire for.
  • Senior leader. Useful for understanding the organization, but rarely the right first contact.
C. What do you do with what you found?
  • Record it. Keep notes on patterns and questions, in the same simple record you use for contacts.
  • Verify it. Confirm anything important, like an opening or a requirement, with the employer or your university.
  • Find the gaps. Questions no profile answers make the best outreach questions.
  • Reach out. When you have a specific reason and question, use Reach Out to Someone New.

Common mistakes

  • Treating one person’s path as the only way in.
  • Inflating titles or responsibilities, or letting your profile and resume disagree.
  • Contacting only the most senior people.
  • Sending requests to many people at one organization at once, with no note.
  • Trusting a posting without checking the employer’s own site.
  • Polishing your profile for hours instead of using it.
  • Asking someone a question their profile already answers.
