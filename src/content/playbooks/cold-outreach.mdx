## The four steps

### 1. Find someone relevant

Look for people one or two steps ahead of you on a path you're considering: alumni, people at organizations on your target list, speakers from events you attended, or people your contacts mention. Alumni directories, professional networking sites, company websites and club events are all good places to look.

A few well-chosen people beat fifty generic messages.

### 2. Research a real reason

Personalization means showing why *this person* is relevant to your question. Mentioning their employer or that they went to USC is not enough on its own: thousands of people share those.

Spend about ten minutes on their career path, anything they've written or said publicly, and their team's work. Look for a specific connection, like a career switch you're considering, a project you have a question about, or a path you're trying to understand. Leave out personal details unrelated to their work.

### 3. Reach out

Keep it short:

1. Who you are, in one line.
2. Why them, in one or two lines.
3. One small ask: a short call or one question.
4. An easy way to say no.

Don't ask a stranger for a job, an internship or a referral. Use email if you can find a professional address; otherwise a message on a professional networking site works.

### 4. Follow through

- **They say yes:** reply within a day, offer a few times, and prepare three or four real questions. After the conversation, use [After a Recruiting Event](../after-a-recruiting-event/) to follow up.
- **No reply:** one short, polite follow-up after about a week is reasonable. Then stop and try someone else.

**Why one follow-up here, but not after an event?** A thank-you note doesn't ask for anything, so it doesn't need a reply. Cold outreach does ask for something, so one gentle reminder is fair. More than one is pressure.

*Illustrative follow-up:* "Hi Alex, I wanted to follow up on my note below in case it got buried. If now isn't a good time, no problem at all. Thank you, Jordan"

### Weak vs. strong

*Illustrative example, not a real exchange.*

**Weak:** "Hi! I'm a USC student interested in consulting. I see you work at a consulting firm and also went to USC. Would you be able to help me get an internship? Let me know when you're free to chat!"

**Stronger:** "Hi Alex, my name is Jordan, a sophomore at USC studying business administration. I'm reaching out because you moved from audit into strategy consulting, and I'm trying to understand how people make that switch. Would you be open to a 15 to 20 minute call in the coming weeks? If now isn't a good time, I completely understand. Thank you for considering it."

The weak version's only "personalization" is their employer and school, and it asks a stranger for a job. The stronger one gives a specific reason tied to their path, makes one small ask, and makes it easy to say no.

## How to adapt this to your situation

The four steps don't change. The message does. Open the sections that match your situation.

<details>
<summary>A. Why are you contacting this person?</summary>

- **To learn about an unfamiliar industry.** Ask how they got in and what surprised them. Do basic research first so your questions go beyond what a quick search would answer. Don't pretend to know more than you do.
- **Because of a specific organization.** Show you've looked into it. Ask about the work and the team, not about open positions.
- **About a specific opening (recruiters).** Name the exact role, add one line on why you're a fit, and ask something they can actually answer, like what they look for. Don't ask them to skip their process.
- **Because they're an alum.** The shared school gets your message opened, but it isn't a reason by itself. You still need "why this person."

</details>

<details>
<summary>B. How strong is your existing connection?</summary>

- **No connection at all.** Lead with relevance, keep it shortest, and make the smallest ask.
- **A shared school, club or group.** Mention it once, early, then move on to your real reason.
- **A mutual contact suggested them.** Name that person in your first line. Check first that they're comfortable with you using their name.
- **A mutual contact could introduce you.** An introduction usually works better than a cold message. Ask your contact, and make it easy: send a short note they can forward, saying who you are and why you'd like to connect.

</details>

<details>
<summary>C. What happens after you reach out?</summary>

- **They say yes.** Reply quickly, make scheduling easy, prepare, and follow up afterward with [After a Recruiting Event](../after-a-recruiting-event/).
- **They say no or not now.** Thank them and keep the door open. Don't argue or push.
- **They point you to someone else.** Contact that person, mention who sent you, and let the first person know how it went.
- **No reply after one follow-up.** Stop. No reply is common and rarely personal. Try someone else.
- **They reply weeks later.** Answer warmly and normally. No need to mention the delay.

</details>

## Common mistakes

- "Personalizing" with only their employer or school.
- Asking a stranger for a job, internship or referral.
- "Can I pick your brain?" with no topic.
- Telling your whole story, or making more than one ask.
- Sending the same message to several people at one organization.
- Asking questions their company website already answers.
- Following up more than once.
