## The four steps

### 1. Record: same day

On the walk home, put these in your phone's notes:

- Name, role and organization
- What you talked about, in a phrase
- One specific thing they said that stuck with you
- Anything either of you said would happen next

Keep these notes on your own device. This site doesn't collect or store them.

### 2. Thank: ideally within 24–48 hours

Send a short, personalized thank-you through the channel you exchanged: email if you got a card or address, LinkedIn if that's how you connected. Mention one real detail. A thoughtful note on day four still beats a generic one sent within the hour.

### 3. Follow through

Do what you said you'd do, and act on any next step they suggested. Following through is what gives you a real reason to write again.

### 4. Reconnect when there's a reason

Write again when you've used their advice, made progress, or found something relevant to what you discussed. A check-in with nothing to say is not a reason. Keep a simple record so you know when to reach out: person, where you met, last contact, next reason.

### Weak vs. strong

*Illustrative example, not a real exchange.*

**Weak:** "Thanks for chatting at the career fair! It was great meeting you and learning about your company. Hope to stay in touch!"

**Stronger:** "Thank you for taking the time to talk with me at the fall career fair. I especially appreciated your advice to talk to people on more than one team before applying. I had only been looking at one, so I'm widening my search. If there is anyone you would recommend I speak with, I would be grateful for the suggestion."

The weak version could go to anyone. The stronger one proves you listened and offers one easy, optional next step.

## How to adapt this to your situation

The four steps don't change. The message does. Open the sections that match your situation.

<details>
<summary>Who you're writing to</summary>

- **Recruiter:** They meet many students. Be brief, name the role or program you discussed, and don't ask for things they can't give.
- **Alum or working professional:** More room for curiosity. A question about their path fits here.
- **Senior leader:** Shortest version. Thank them and mention one detail. No ask.
- **Fellow student:** Keep it casual and offer something back, like a resource or an upcoming event.

</details>

<details>
<summary>A. How substantial was the conversation?</summary>

- **Brief, at a crowded event.** Thank them and mention one detail. Skip the ask, and don't write as if you know each other well. If you honestly can't recall a specific detail, refer to the topic in general terms. Never invent one.
- **A real, memorable conversation.** Show you listened by connecting what they said to something you're doing. One specific question or a request for a recommendation is reasonable.

</details>

<details>
<summary>B. Was a next step discussed? <span class="summary-note">Includes introductions and referrals</span></summary>

- **They offered to help.** Take them up on it soon, while the offer is fresh. Name one specific, small thing (a short call, one question), and make it easy for them to say no.
- **They suggested someone to contact.** Reach out to that person and say who suggested it. Then tell the original contact how it went. Closing the loop is how you earn the next suggestion.
- **No next step came up.** Don't invent one. Thank them, and at most ask whether they'd recommend a person or resource, or say you'd like to stay in touch.
- **You want an introduction or a referral.** These are different. An *introduction* connects you with someone. A *referral* is a recommendation for a specific opening, and at some employers it's a formal process. Before asking for a referral, check three things:
  1. Does this person understand the role you're targeting?
  2. Do they know enough about your background to recommend you credibly?
  3. Could they say no without feeling awkward?

  If any answer is no, start smaller: ask for advice, then an introduction. A friendly conversation doesn't oblige anyone to refer you, and every employer handles referrals differently.

</details>

<details>
<summary>C. What happened after your first message?</summary>

- **They replied.** Answer briefly, act on what they said, and later tell them how you used it.
- **They didn't reply.** That's normal and isn't a rejection. Don't nudge them about the same message. Write again only when you have a new, real reason.
- **Weeks or months have passed.** Still worth reaching out if you have a reason. In one line, remind them where you met, then share the update or question. No long apology, and don't act closer than you are.
- **You can't find their contact information.** A LinkedIn connection request with a short note works. If you can't find them, let it go.

</details>

## Common mistakes

- Sending the same message to everyone you met. Colleagues may compare notes.
- Acting more familiar than you are ("Great catching up!" after a two-minute chat).
- Asking for a job, referral or interview in the first message.
- Attaching your resume when nobody asked for it.
- Writing too much. If it needs scrolling on a phone, cut it.
- Flattering ("Your talk was amazing!") instead of being specific.
- Following up again and again when they haven't replied.
